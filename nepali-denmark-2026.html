Nepali Denmark in 2026 - The Surge, the Suburbs and the Second Generation

November 09, 2026 • Vibek Raj Maurya

Last year I tried to tell the story of the Nepali community in Denmark with a handful of tables from Statistics Denmark. The headline then was 12,444 people in early 2025, up from barely a thousand in 2008.

Since then I have gone back to Statbank, pulled a much deeper cut of the data, and rebuilt the whole thing as an interactive story. The headline has changed: 15,408 people by the third quarter of 2026. But the more interesting changes are underneath the total - who is arriving, where they settle, and what a community that is starting to put down roots looks like.

Thirteen times in eighteen years

In 2008, 1,174 people of Nepali origin lived in Denmark. By the end of September 2026 there were 15,408. That is a thirteenfold increase, or roughly +1,212% in under two decades.

That single line hides two very different periods.

1,174 → 15,408
People of Nepali origin in Denmark, 2008 to 2026 Q3 · Source: Statistics Denmark, FOLK1C

Between 2008 and 2016 the community more than tripled, from 1,174 to 4,089. Then came the strange years.

The plateau, and the bend

From 2017 the curve flattened. Between 2018 and 2020 the population barely moved, and in 2019 it actually fell by 24 people - the only yearly decline in the entire series. COVID-19 then cut immigration to a trickle: just 234 Nepali citizens arrived in 2020.

What happened next is the part I did not see coming last year:

Year Population (year-end) Change
2020 4,976 +132
2021 5,577 +601
2022 6,765 +1,188
2023 8,839 +2,074
2024 11,471 +2,632
2025 15,110 +3,639 (+31.7%)

Five consecutive years of accelerating growth, each one bigger than the last. 2025 alone added more people than the entire community numbered in 2015. The curve did not just recover after the pandemic - it changed slope.

One caveat on the numbers: the last point, 2026, is a Q3 snapshot rather than a year-end figure, so treat it as a partial year. Even so, it is already 298 people above the 2025 year-end total.

Nine in ten come to study

Why is this happening? Statbank also records the residence permit each immigrant arrives on, and for Nepali citizens the answer is overwhelming.

Of the 3,073 Nepali citizens who immigrated to Denmark in 2025, 2,804 - about 91% - came on a study-related permit. That category bundles higher education with au pair placements, interns, and the family members who come with them. The student pipeline is not part of the story of Nepali migration to Denmark; it essentially is the story. You can see the same pattern as far back as 2007, when 349 of 397 arrivals were students.

The second channel is smaller but worth watching. Nepali citizens admitted under EU/EEA rules are almost entirely dependent family members - spouses and children of an EU/EEA national - rather than EU workers or students. That group was in single digits until 2019, peaked at 454 in 2022, and stood at 182 in 2025. It reads like family reunification following the students, not a separate labour migration.

Work permits, by contrast, remain tiny: 51 in 2025. Whatever else Denmark is to Nepalis, it is not primarily a jobs destination.

A young community, slowly settling

The age profile explains almost everything else. In 2026, people aged 20 to 34 made up 55% of the community. The single largest band is 20-24, at 24.4% of the total - exactly what you would expect from a migration stream built on universities.

But look at the edges of the pyramid and a different story appears. Children aged 0-9 are now 13% of the community, and the 35-44 bands have grown from a small slice to nearly a fifth. Students who arrived in the 2010s are staying, working, and having children.

The sex ratio is also evening out. There are 8,158 men and 7,250 women - a 53/47 split, compared with the heavily male-skewed early years when student migration dominated.

A second generation appears

The clearest sign of settlement is in a category Statistics Denmark calls descendants: people born in Denmark to parents born in Nepal.

In 2008 there were 32 of them. By 2026 there are 1,657 - about one in ten people of Nepali origin in the country. This is no longer a community made only of people who arrived; it is one that is beginning to be born here.

That shift matters for how the community is understood. A student who returns home after a degree and a child who grows up Danish are both "Nepali Denmark", but they are not the same thing, and the second group is the one that will define the next twenty years.

Almost everyone lives in Greater Copenhagen

The geography is striking. 83.6% of the community lives in Region Hovedstaden, the capital region. Add Region Sjælland and you account for almost nine in ten people.

But within that, the growth has moved outward. Copenhagen municipality still has the largest community at 3,369 people, up from 598 in 2008. The real explosion, however, happened in the suburbs west of the city:

Municipality 2008 2026 Q3
Copenhagen 598 3,369
Høje-Taastrup 12 1,851
Ishøj 3 1,317
Brøndby 5 844
Glostrup 12 790
Rødovre 11 708
Vallensbæk 3 664
Herlev 0 620

Høje-Taastrup went from a dozen people to the second-largest Nepali community in the country. Ishøj from three to over a thousand. These are not the inner-city neighbourhoods people usually associate with new migration - they are the cheaper, family-sized suburbs within reach of the capital.

Outside the Copenhagen region the numbers are much smaller: Odense has 369, Aalborg 211, Aarhus 188, Esbjerg 43. On Bornholm there are 20 people, and on Christiansø, the tiny fortress island, exactly zero.

Becoming Danish

The final step in settling is citizenship. It stayed a trickle for decades - a handful a year through the 2000s - and then climbed sharply after 2020.

In total, 693 people of Nepali origin have acquired Danish citizenship since 1979. Of those, 114 did so in 2024 and 94 in 2025 - against single digits or low teens through most of the 2000s and early 2010s. The age profile of new citizens is split neatly in two: young children naturalising alongside their parents, and adults in their late thirties and forties who have lived here long enough to qualify.

What the numbers cannot tell you

A word of caution, because this data is easy to over-read.

The population figures are counts of people of Nepali origin - born in Nepal, or born in Denmark to parents born in Nepal - regardless of citizenship. The migration figures, on the other hand, are counts of Nepali citizens crossing the border. They are two different populations, and mixing them (as my earlier post partly did) can produce misleading totals. The permit data also counts arrivals, not people who stayed: a student who graduates and leaves still appears in the immigration figures.

So the numbers show a community growing fast and settling. They do not show how many stay, what they work with, or how the second generation will identify. That would need different data.

Data & method

All figures come from Statistics Denmark's Statbank. Population stock is table FOLK1C (population by country of origin, age, sex, ancestry, region and municipality). Migration flows come from Immigrations by citizenship, sex and residence permit, and naturalisations from Persons who acquired Danish citizenship by former citizenship. Every year-point is a Q4 year-end observation except the latest, 2026 Q3. The raw download was split into three batches to respect Statbank's record limit and merged before analysis.
